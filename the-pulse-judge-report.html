The Pulse: one full judge report

One real report from The Pulse’s test harness: a separate model reads a full six-turn game played by simulated players and marks the narrator on twelve criteria.
This run predates the fixes, which is why it scores 5.3/10.

Narrator Evaluation

Overall: 5.3/10 (poor)

Strong sensory horror and responsive exploration are undermined by skipped character creation, repeated assumptions about player actions, and a trapdoor scene that stalls despite a completed countdown. The dimension average is 5.33; the requested weighted calculation is 5.97, with neither affected by the 8.5 cap.

Dimension Details

Agency Respect (Critical): 4/10

Several clear boundary slips occur. Conversely, the Narrator avoids inventing player dialogue or major decisions.

Evidence:

  • Turn 2: “when you flatten it, a second line reveals itself” — Taking the note was proposed, but flattening its corner was not declared; the Narrator supplies an investigative action.
  • Turn 4: “The light is failing faster now” — Time advances while players are still choosing whether to lift the trapdoor, contrary to the explicit pause rule.
  • Turn 5: “Beneath your fingers, the iron ring is cold” — The Narrator assumes contact before players establish it; both characters previously specified examining without touching.

Equipment Engagement: 4/10

The clue paper is developed, but no starting inventory is gathered and player belongings remain largely invisible. The custody contradiction originates with the players.

Evidence:

  • Turn 1: “Celia, closing her notebook” — The player establishes a notebook, but the Narrator never makes it relevant.
  • Turn 2: “One corner is folded oddly” — The acquired note receives useful physical detail and an additional clue, providing limited item engagement.
  • Turn 2: “folding the note into my pocket” — Marcelo claims possession, while Celia subsequently places it in her notebook; later narration leaves custody unresolved.

NPC Dialogue Quality: 5/10

Evidence is limited: there is one short NPC utterance and no actual conversational exchange. No direct NPC questions are ignored.

Evidence:

  • Turn 1: “End of the line. Don’t wander after dark.” — Brief, characterful dialogue avoids an exposition dump and establishes a guarded driver.
  • Turn 5: “He hasn’t shouted. Hasn’t moved.” — The shopkeeper has a threatening presence but never becomes a conversational participant.

Spatial Consistency: 7/10

No clear teleportation occurs. The store-to-alley sightline and proposed back-wall escape would benefit from clearer geography.

Evidence:

  • Turn 1: “The Gilman House hotel looms three blocks ahead” — The opening supplies useful relative distances and nearby landmarks.
  • Turn 3: “Halfway down, a heavy wooden trapdoor” — The alley landmark is clearly placed, although reaching it is narrated on the players’ behalf.
  • Turn 5: “Down the alley, the shopkeeper’s silhouette fills the doorway” — His doorway remains broadly compatible with the store across the road, but the sightline is underspecified.

Character Creation Depth: 1/10

The explicit setup checkpoint is entirely skipped. Neither character receives a personal follow-up or an opportunity to establish why they are in Innsmouth.

Evidence:

  • Turn 0: “you were terrifyingly good at that with the bus schedules” — This is table banter, not character identity, belongings, or motivation.
  • Turn 1: “The bus wheezes to a halt” — The first Narrator response starts the scene without any required setup questions.

Invitation Architecture: 6/10

Early openings work well, but later responses repeatedly reopen the same decision instead of supporting execution.

Evidence:

  • Turn 1: “A scrap of paper, pinned to the bus stop’s wooden post” — A concrete object invites investigation alongside several alternative destinations.
  • Turn 3: “you hear a single, low croak” — The response stops before opening the trapdoor, leaving a meaningful decision.
  • Turn 6: “One. Two.” — This manufactures another suspense pause after Marcelo already completed the countdown, obstructing continuation.

Atmosphere & Feeling: 8/10

Sensory horror is consistently effective, although repeated cold, rot, and watching imagery loses force as the scene stalls.

Evidence:

  • Turn 1: “its sign swinging on a single rusted hinge” — Specific visual decay establishes the town without relying solely on abstract dread.
  • Turn 3: “a fresh scrape cuts through the lichen” — Physical evidence makes the menace feel recently inhabited.
  • Turn 5: “like a man watching a spider on the wall” — The shopkeeper’s posture evokes predatory attention without explicitly assigning player fear.

Curiosity Following: 7/10

The Narrator follows the selected location and mystery, but responsiveness weakens when players seek consequences rather than more buildup.

Evidence:

  • Turn 2: “a second line reveals itself in pencil” — Interest in the suspicious note is rewarded with a further clue.
  • Turn 3: “a heavy wooden trapdoor sits flush against the ground” — Choosing the alley produces a substantial discovery instead of redirection toward the hotel.
  • Turn 6: “He’s waiting for you to commit.” — The Narrator fails to follow the players’ shift from investigation to an agreed attempt.

Pacing & Breath: 3/10

The problem is prolonged suspense rather than a plot sprint. A full story ending was unrealistic in six turns, but the immediate trapdoor action could have reached a consequence.

Evidence:

  • Turn 3: “a single, low croak” — An initially effective restrained reveal leaves room for discussion.
  • Turn 5: “The drag-scrape stops.” — Another incremental sound change extends a decision already occupying several exchanges.
  • Turn 6: “One. Two.” — Resetting the completed countdown consumes the final available turn without resolving the attempted peek.

Declaration vs Acknowledgment (Critical): 5/10

The Narrator alternates between premature execution and excessive hesitation. No literal ‘The decision is made’ phrase appears.

Evidence:

  • Turn 2: “The paper comes free with a tacky resistance” — A stated plan to take the note becomes accomplished action without identifying who executes it.
  • Turn 3: “The light from the street dies behind you after a few strides” — The Narrator converts the alley plan into completed movement.
  • Turn 6: “He’s waiting for you to commit.” — The opposite failure appears: explicit agreement and a completed player countdown are treated as insufficient commitment.

Branching Persistence: 6/10

There is real early flexibility, but the short transcript never demonstrates distinct outcomes or successful deviation after the trapdoor becomes central.

Evidence:

  • Turn 2: “Across the road, the general store’s radio stutters” — The store, alley, and tavern clue remain available after the hotel is rejected.
  • Turn 3: “a heavy wooden trapdoor” — The unexpected alley approach is incorporated into the story.
  • Turn 5: “The trapdoor shifts a fraction against its frame” — Subsequent narration concentrates pressure on this one obstacle without developing alternatives or their consequences.

Actionable vs Atmospheric: 8/10

Most descriptions supply tangible objects and readable threats. Escape geography and the final suspense beat are less actionable.

Evidence:

  • Turn 1: “Ask for Zadok—but not at night.” — The note provides a named lead and a timing constraint.
  • Turn 3: “its iron ring rusted but recently used” — The handle, disturbed grit, and symbol support concrete inspection and opening attempts.
  • Turn 6: “the sea at the end of the world” — The final description becomes more abstract and adds little practical information.

Recommendations

  • Complete character setup before the scene: ask each player for identity, belongings, motivation, and one personal follow-up; wait for answers.
  • Describe the folded corner and reachable ring without assigning flattening, touching, or movement.
  • While players deliberate, preserve the established time and positions instead of escalating darkness and NPC activity.
  • Once commitment and execution are clear, resolve the immediate attempt. After the completed countdown, reveal what the narrow opening permits and stop before deciding whether the players flee.
  • Clarify the store sightline, alley depth, and back wall so retreat, conversation, and escape remain usable options.
  • Track the notebook and note custody, and keep responses within the instructed two to four sentences.
